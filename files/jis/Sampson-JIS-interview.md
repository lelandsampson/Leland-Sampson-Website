# What I offer to Learned Hand

### Early love of technology

As far back as I can remember I have LOVED technology. My father bought our first computer around 1996. It was a Gateway 2000. I remember it was a Pentium 133 mhz. I eventually took it apart many years later to discover it didn't even have a CPU fan. There was not a lot of software for a kid to use, but I would go play on the PC every day after school and use MS Paint to draw a battleship. At the time I was also obsessed with the Navy and wanted to either be an Admiral or an over the road truck driver.

Anyway I loved tinkering on the PC and desperately wanted my own to have in my room. There was a tv network called TechTV that I also watched nearly every day. There was a personality called Leo Laporte who was so entertaining. He hosted a show called Call for Help where people would call in with tech problems and they'd help you fix it. But at 7pm was a live show called the ScreenSavers with Leo and Patrick Norton.  I think it was for my tenth birthday, my dad took me to a PC show. In those days there were PC conventions where you could buy a barebones PC and other bits and pieces, put it together, install Windows and build your own at a discount. So my dad bought me a barebones package that had an AMD Thunderbird Slot A processor and a 3dfx Voodoo 2. I installed Windows 98 on the PC, managed to get Castlewolfenstein 3d running. It scared me so I think I only played it a handful of times. And after that computers just became my hobby. Summer time was the best because I had a friend who also loved computers and we'd spend all day at eachother's houses playing multiplayer and pirating music off Kazaa and LimeWire. My dad would drop me off at the Barnes and Noble every Wednesday with $20 and I'd go and get a latte at starbucks and a copy of every computer magazine they had and spend 2 hours reading and drinking coffee. I really miss those old school computer magazines, they were such a part of my childhood. I also didn't realize it at the time, but collecting and organizing information was another hobby/passion of mine that dovetailed so nicely with my PC collection. When Netflix was a DVD service, I worked out a system where I could get 6 discs per week and rip them for my collection. I eventually digitized them after hard drive storage became cheaper than having the physical discs around. That DVD collection is still the basis of my Plex collection 20 years later. Alphabetizing my collection was very soothing for me. I have always had a custom built PC. I love picking the motherboard, CPU, ram, graphics card, storage, powersupply. I also love working on laptops. I've never bought a laptop new from a store. I have always bought broken laptops on ebay to use. There is a really wonderful ecosystem of business laptops that come off lease from big companies then they sell those off lease machines to wholesalers on ebay. I've probably bought a dozen thinkpads over the years, those were the machines I used all through college and law school. I really wish I had kept it, but my favorite laptop was a Thinkpad t420. It was a dream to work on, built like a tank. The only component that I did not replace on that machine was the chassis, everything else was rebuilt at some point.



### College and law school

My father is an attorney and my grandfather was an attorney. So I always thought I wanted to be an attorney. This was good and bad. It was good in the sense that from a very young age I had a goal to work toward. I think goal's are important. The bad was that I structured everything I did in high school and college around getting in to law school. My father would always say "I'm terrible at math." So I assumed I would also be terrible at math. So I avoided math as best I could thinking it would sink my GPA and would be the reason I wouldn't get into law school. This risk aversion plays a big part in my current professional situation, but more on that later. I took latin in high school on the theory it might be useful in law. Thankfully I did take latin because I met my future wife in that class. I found college so much better than high school. In my freshman year my favorite class was symbolic logic. I absolutely loved working on logical proofs. After understanding the rules and the operations it was fun to puzzle over the proof, though stressful at times. The key with incorporating large language models responsibly is going to be taking deterministic workflows and best utilizing the creativity of the LLM. A key will be distilling pleadings down to their semantic blocks and using connectors to distill the argument and ensure the meaning is preserved through the large language model's probabilistic creativity.



Anyway, getting back to college. I majored in going to law school - political science and philosophy. The symbolic logic class was part of my philosophy study. The other philosophy class I really enjoyed was theory of knowledge. Logically proving what we know has greatly shaped the way I try to reason about novel problems. Structure of arguments is also of great interest to me. There is a really great introduction to logical argument and reasoning called \[An Illustrated Book of Bad Arguments](https://bookofbadarguments.com/) that is fantastic at describing the structure of how mad arguments are presented in daily life and how to spot them. I highly recommend it. I keep a bookshelf of critically important books my kids need to read and that has prime placement on that shelf. In my junior year of college I took a course from a retired lawyer who was pursing his PHd at Maryland, Michael Spivey. The first thing we read in that class was Lon Fuller's law review article \[The Case of the Speluncean Explorers](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1336025). The debate that the article spurred among the five people in the class who actually read the article was awesome. I discovered I was so naive. At one point I accused Spivey of being disingenuous, and he politely told me that that was a nice way of calling someone a liar. The debates that he and I had in that class were easily the highlight of my college career.



During college I worked as an intern at the Circuit Court for Anne Arundel County. It was my local trial court and I wanted to get a good resume booster. There were no internship postings, but I wondered around the courthouse until I found the case management office. There were three folks there who seemed open to having an intern and they told me to come back next week for an interview. The interviewers were Tim Sheridan, now the court administrator for Montgomery County Circuit Court, and Nancy Faulkner, the current Deputy State Court Administrator. They "hired" me, even though I was working for free. So during the summers in college I'd work at the circuit court a few days a week, then go work at the golf course and/or cigar store to earn some cash.



My 1L year at UB I did full time, but still had my face in at the circuit court. A job opened up as an ADR coordinator - a grant funded position. So I decided to switch to night law school and start working full time at the Anne Arundel Circuit Court. Eventually a normal salaried position as a paralegal opened up and I worked there until I finished law school. As my law school education was winding down in 2015, I saw a job posting for an administrative assistant with Pam Harris, the Maryland State Court Administrator. I wasn't entirely sure what I wanted to do in law at this point, I didn't think I wanted to practice. I turned down a law clerk position at the trial court to apply for this administrative aide position. There was a retired judge, Diane Leasure who I worked with at the circuit court on a pretrial settlement conference docket. I also took Judge Leasure's classes at UMD law in the evenings. She sent Pam an email and Pam gave me an interview. I then got called in for an interview with Pam and the Deputy State Court Administrator at the time, Faye Gaskin. Pam ended up creating a new position for me. My initial title was Administrative Aide, but there was another person hired at the same time who was the real administrative aide. My role was to act like Pam's lawyer. The first project she had me work on was combing through Maryland's statutes and administrative orders to figure out the exact responsibilities that the state court administrator was charged with fulfilling. Then I worked on projects. I helped entirely re-write the Judiciary's employee handbook. I was in charge of the legislatively mandated reports the Judiciary had to send to the legislature. I helped Pam and Chief Judge Barbera rescind and reissue all the administrative orders. I sat in on court technology committee meetings and told Pam when IT was bullshitting her, what types of on the ground problems were going on in the Anne Arundel clerk's office. I was at Anne Arundel when they rolled out the Tyler Technology Odyssey case management system. Pam was a tremendous mentor and really gave me a passion for the role that active case management can play in the administration of justice.



### Move to the law library

While working for Pam a job opened up at the state law library that seemed like a perfect fit for me: managing the People's Law Library website. It combined my two passions: law and technology. I learned so many important skills there. I learned how to manage an IT project from procurement to go-live. I learned how to do real legal research, how to do intensive legislative history research, how to work with proprietary databases and work understand the research needs of judges. I thought I wanted to be the library director, so I worked my way through a master of library science degree. But then the artificial intelligence revolution started. Because I love technology, AI research and application has been my passion for the last several years. I've tried to make a name for myself as an expert in the field through presentations, getting on committees and getting my name out there. I really enjoy teaching people how the technology works, how to use it responsibly, and how it can augment the skills of lawyers. In the spring of 2023 I developed a system called Ferritit that was an early retrieval augmented generation chatbot. See \[slides 101-105 of my presentation for the American Bar Association in 2023](https://sampson.info/files/2023-aba-iolta-conference-tech-ai-slides.pdf). I was using ChatGPT to vibe code before that had a name. For my vibe code example \[see slides 52-86 of my presentation at the 2024 American Association of Law Librarians Annual Conference](/files/2024-AALL-Practical-AI.pdf). Vibe Coding was coined in 2025 and I was doing it in 2023 and 2024 before we had fancy harnesses like Claude Code and Codex.







### What I bring to Judicial Information Systems

I have worked for a state judiciary for nearly 15 years. I have worked at a trial court, in the administrative office, worked with appellate judges and judiciary leaders on committees. I have extensive experience with case management systems as a court employee, I help lawyers and self-represented litigants with e-filing. I understand the technology, the courts, and the law. What I want to do now is build the AI tools. I want to bring my knowledge, skills, and experience to JIS to build great tools to help courts and judges advance the administration of justice. Federal courts are starting to see the influx of AI generated pleadings. State courts are not far behind. At a National Association of Court Management session on SRL's use of AI, clerks were complaining of the influx of badly generated AI pleadings. I've been trying to warn the Maryland Judiciary for years that the clear and present danger is not going to be deep fake evidence, it's going to be a tsunami of filings from litigants that we are not prepared to deal with. State courts are not built for a 300% increase in filings, but that is exactly what they are going to get in the coming years. JIS can build the solution to this tsunami of filings. I see that and I desperately want to lead that project. I think it's absolutely critical to the Judiciary's mission. I want to be part of this exciting future!
